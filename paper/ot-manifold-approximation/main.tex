\title{\textbf{Wasserstein Cartography of Gaussian Decoders}\\
\large A Regularity Hierarchy for Latent Metrics, Distances, and Curvature}
\author{Anonymous Authors}
\date{}

\begin{document}

\begin{abstract}
A probabilistic decoder may fit its observations accurately while inducing the
wrong geometry on its latent space.  We make this gap quantitative for diagonal
Gaussian decoders equipped with the 2-Wasserstein distance.  The Gaussian
closed form turns the decoder into a Euclidean parameter immersion
$F=(m,\sigma)$, whose pullback metric is $G=J_F^{\trans}J_F$.  Our main result
establishes a regularity hierarchy: uniform first-derivative error controls the
metric and every intrinsic distance multiplicatively, whereas curvature
requires uniform second-derivative control.  Both requirements are necessary.
We also show that noisy local $W_2^2$ measurements recover the metric with the
bias--noise tradeoff $O(h^2+\eta/h^2)$, yielding $O(\sqrt\eta)$ accuracy at the
optimal probe scale.
We construct Gaussian decoders that converge uniformly without metric
convergence, and decoders that converge in $C^1$ while their Gaussian curvature
does not converge.  In particular, a piecewise-affine ReLU parameter decoder is
flat almost everywhere, regardless of its distributional fit.

We introduce a controlled ``Wasserstein cartography'' benchmark in which a
Mercator chart hides a unit sphere behind a learned family of Gaussian
distributions.  The exact solution has metric $\sech^2(y)I$, curvature one, and
an analytic distinction between the global Wasserstein chord and the intrinsic
transport path.  Over five independent trials, a smooth Tanh decoder reaches
metric relative error $0.075\pm0.008$, curvature MAE $0.140\pm0.016$, and
geodesic RMSE $0.021\pm0.003$.  A ReLU decoder obtains nearly the same held-out
likelihood but curvature MAE $1.000\pm0.000$.  The results show that
reconstruction, distance, and curvature are different statistical targets, and
identify the derivative accuracy needed for each.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Latent variables are coordinates, not geometry.  Reparameterizing a latent
space can drastically change Euclidean distances while leaving the generated
distribution unchanged.  Pullback Riemannian geometry addresses this problem
by measuring a latent displacement through the decoder
\cite{arvanitidis2018,chen2018}.  The resulting geodesics have been used for
interpolation, clustering, motion synthesis, and geometry-aware generation.

A fundamental issue remains.  A decoder is trained to fit distributions or
samples, but its metric depends on first derivatives and its curvature on
second derivatives.  Small reconstruction or likelihood error therefore need
not imply small geometric error.  This mismatch is particularly consequential
for modern piecewise-linear networks: they may approximate a smooth decoder
well in value while having a qualitatively different second-order geometry.

We study this issue in the simplest nontrivial optimal-transport setting.  Let
\[
 K_z=\mathcal N\!\left(m(z),\diag(\sigma(z)^2)\right),
 \qquad z\in\cZ\subset\R^d,
\]
be a diagonal Gaussian decoder.  The exact Gaussian formula gives
\[
 \W(K_z,K_{z'})^2
 =\norm{m(z)-m(z')}^2+\norm{\sigma(z)-\sigma(z')}^2.
\]
Thus $F=(m,\sigma)$ embeds the decoder family into a Euclidean space, and
local optimal-transport cost induces the pullback metric
$G=J_F^{\trans}J_F$.  This setting isolates the learning question without
requiring an approximation to the ambient Wasserstein metric.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item We prove explicit stability bounds from decoder derivative error to the
  pullback metric, all intrinsic distances, the second fundamental form, and
  Gaussian curvature.  In short, $C^1$ accuracy controls distance and $C^2$
  accuracy controls curvature.
  \item We give a central local-transport estimator for the full metric tensor.
  Under squared-distance error $\eta$, its error is
  $O(h^2+\eta/h^2)$ and is minimized at spatial scale $h\asymp\eta^{1/4}$.
  \item We prove that the hierarchy is necessary through two smooth
  counterexamples inside the class of Gaussian decoders.  We also formalize the
  almost-everywhere flatness of piecewise-affine parameter decoders.
  \item We connect the global Wasserstein chord to intrinsic transport through
  a curvature-controlled cubic bound.  This distinguishes direct transport
  between endpoint distributions from paths constrained to the decoder family.
  \item We provide a fully reproducible benchmark with known metric, curvature,
  geodesics, and surface.  Unlike a generic synthetic point cloud, every plotted
  object has an explicit statistical input and a unique evaluation target.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

The pullback geometry of deep generative models was developed for deterministic
and stochastic decoders by Arvanitidis et al.~\cite{arvanitidis2018} and Chen
et al.~\cite{chen2018}.  For Gaussian decoders, their expected stochastic
metric has the same expression $J_m^{\trans}J_m+J_\sigma^{\trans}J_\sigma$ that
we derive directly from $W_2$.  Later work regularizes this metric toward
flatness \cite{chen2020}, enriches the ambient metric with domain knowledge
\cite{arvanitidis2021}, approximates it using the latent prior
\cite{arvanitidis2022prior}, or replaces the ambient geometry with Fisher--Rao
geometry for more general distributions \cite{arvanitidis2022fisher}.  Recent
methods use decoder ensembles \cite{syrota2024}, Riemannian data spaces
\cite{rozo2025}, and score models \cite{diepeveen2025}.  These works construct
or exploit latent metrics.  We instead ask which mode of decoder convergence is
sufficient for the learned metric and curvature themselves to converge.

Most directly, Roy and Hauberg's \emph{Optimal Latent Transport}
\cite{roy2022} pulls Wasserstein distances between discrete decoder
distributions back to a VAE latent space and computes geodesics by optimizing
discretized curve length.  Their question is computational: given a trained
decoder, which path does its Wasserstein geometry induce?  Our question is
complementary and statistical: when an estimated decoder approximates a true
one, at which derivative order do the induced metric, intrinsic distances, and
curvature become reliable?  In the diagonal-Gaussian $W_2$ setting, this leads
to an explicit metric tensor, quantitative stability bounds, necessary
counterexamples, and a finite-scale noisy recovery guarantee.
This does not subsume their discrete $W_1$ construction; it supplies reliability
guarantees and higher-order geometry in a different, analytically tractable
decoder class.

Graph shortest paths estimate manifold geodesics from local ambient distances,
as in Isomap \cite{tenenbaum2000}.  Our local edge costs are not generic
Euclidean proxies: they are exact $W_2$ distances between decoder distributions.
We retain the distinction between this global chord metric and the path metric
it induces on the decoder family.  Riemannian diffusion models
\cite{debortoli2022} illustrate a downstream setting in which recovering the
correct intrinsic geometry is operationally important.

The Gaussian $W_2$ formula goes back to Givens and Shortt
\cite{givensshortt1984}; the geometry of the full Gaussian family is studied by
Takatsu \cite{takatsu2011}.  We follow the Wasserstein notation of
Villani \cite{villani2009} and restrict the main results to diagonal covariance,
where the parameter immersion and all constants are transparent.

\section{Wasserstein Pullback Geometry}
\label{sec:setup}

Let $\cZ\subset\R^d$ be connected and let
$m:\cZ\to\R^n$ and $\sigma:\cZ\to(0,\infty)^n$.  Define
\[
 F(z):=(m(z),\sigma(z))\in\R^{2n}.
\]
For diagonal Gaussians, the common-noise optimal coupling yields the following
identity.

\begin{proposition}[Gaussian Wasserstein immersion]
\label{prop:w2-immersion}
For every $z,z'\in\cZ$,
\begin{equation}
 \W(K_z,K_{z'})=\norm{F(z)-F(z')}.
 \label{eq:w2-chord}
\end{equation}
If $F$ is $C^1$ and $\rank J_F(z)=d$, the infinitesimal metric induced by
\eqref{eq:w2-chord} is
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 G(z)&=J_F(z)^{\trans}J_F(z)\\
 &=J_m(z)^{\trans}J_m(z)
   +J_\sigma(z)^{\trans}J_\sigma(z).
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:pullback}
\end{equation}
In particular,
\[
 \W(K_z,K_{z+h})^2=h^{\trans}G(z)h+o(\norm{h}^2).
\]
\end{proposition}

The identity follows coordinatewise from the one-dimensional Gaussian
transport map.  The expansion follows by differentiating $F$.  We include the
statement to fix the geometry; a self-contained derivation appears in the
companion seminar notes.

\subsection{Noisy Local Metric Recovery}

Proposition~\ref{prop:w2-immersion} defines $G$ infinitesimally.  The following
result makes the approximation scale explicit and uses only local endpoint
distances.  It also shows why taking arbitrarily short edges is harmful when
transport measurements are noisy.

\begin{proposition}[Central Wasserstein metric probe]
\label{prop:local-probe}
Let $F$ be $C^3$, let $v\in\R^d$ be a unit vector, and suppose
$z\pm hv\in\cZ$.  Assume along this segment that
\[
 \norm{J_F}\le M_1,
 \qquad \norm{D^3F[v,v,v]}\le M_3.
\]
Suppose $\widetilde D^2(z+hv,z-hv)$ estimates
$\W(K_{z+hv},K_{z-hv})^2$ with absolute error at most $\eta$.  Define
\[
 \widetilde Q_h(z;v)
 :=\frac{\widetilde D^2(z+hv,z-hv)}{4h^2}.
\]
Then
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 \abs{\widetilde Q_h(z;v)-v^{\trans}G(z)v}
 &\le \frac{M_1M_3}{3}h^2+\frac{M_3^2}{36}h^4\\
 &\quad+\frac{\eta}{4h^2}.
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:local-probe}
\end{equation}
In particular, the leading error is $O(h^2+\eta/h^2)$ and choosing
$h\asymp\eta^{1/4}$ gives $O(\sqrt\eta)$ error.  In two dimensions, evaluating
$\widetilde Q_h$ at $e_1,e_2$ and
$e_\pm=(e_1\pm e_2)/\sqrt2$ recovers every metric entry via
\begin{gather*}
 \widehat G_{11}=\widetilde Q_h(e_1),\qquad
 \widehat G_{22}=\widetilde Q_h(e_2),\\
 \widehat G_{12}=
 \frac{\widetilde Q_h(e_+)-\widetilde Q_h(e_-)}{2}.
\end{gather*}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Symmetric Taylor expansion gives
\begin{align*}
 F(z+hv)-F(z-hv)&=2hJ_F(z)v+r_h,\\
 \norm{r_h}&\le M_3h^3/3.
\end{align*}
After division by $2h$, the remainder has norm at most $M_3h^2/6$.
Expanding the squared norm and using \eqref{eq:w2-chord} yields the first two
terms in \eqref{eq:local-probe}; the measurement error contributes
$\eta/(4h^2)$.  The polarization identities follow from
$Q(v)=v^{\trans}Gv$.
\end{proof}

For a piecewise $C^1$ curve $c:[0,1]\to\cZ$, define
\begin{align*}
 L_G(c)&=\int_0^1
 \sqrt{\dot c(t)^{\trans}G(c(t))\dot c(t)}\dd t,\\
 d_G(z,z')&=\inf_{c(0)=z,\,c(1)=z'}L_G(c).
\end{align*}
The chain rule shows that $L_G(c)$ is exactly the Euclidean length of
$F\circ c$.  Therefore
\begin{equation}
 \W(K_z,K_{z'})\le d_G(z,z').
 \label{eq:chord-intrinsic}
\end{equation}
The left side permits a straight chord through distributions outside the
decoder family; the right side constrains the entire path to $F(\cZ)$.

When $d=2$ and $F$ is $C^2$, let
\[
 P=J_FG^{-1}J_F^{\trans},\qquad
 B_{ij}=(I-P)\partial_i\partial_jF.
\]
Here $P$ projects onto the tangent space and $B$ is the second fundamental
form.  The Gauss equation gives
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal K_F
 =\frac{\langle B_{11},B_{22}\rangle-\norm{B_{12}}^2}{\det G}.
 \label{eq:gauss}
\end{equation}
Equations \eqref{eq:pullback} and \eqref{eq:gauss} expose the central learning
issue: values determine the fitted distributions, first derivatives determine
distance, and second derivatives determine curvature.

\section{Accuracy for Latent Geometry}
\label{sec:theory}

We now quantify the derivative hierarchy.  Matrix norms without a subscript
are Euclidean operator norms.  For a tensor of second derivatives, maxima are
taken over the two coordinate indices.

\begin{theorem}[Stability of learned geometry]
\label{thm:stability}
Let $d=2$ and let $F,\widehat F:\cZ\to\R^N$ be $C^2$ maps.  Assume $F$ is an
immersion and, uniformly on $\cZ$,
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \sigma_{\min}(J_F)&\ge s>0,
 &\norm{J_F}&\le M_1,\\
 \max_{i,j}\norm{\partial_i\partial_jF}&\le M_2.
 \end{aligned}
\]
Set
\begin{align*}
 \varepsilon_1&:=\sup_z\norm{J_{\widehat F}(z)-J_F(z)},\\
 \varepsilon_2&:=\max_{i,j}\sup_z
 \norm{\partial_i\partial_j\widehat F(z)-\partial_i\partial_jF(z)}.
\end{align*}
Suppose $\varepsilon_1<s$ and define
\begin{align*}
 q&:=2M_1\varepsilon_1+\varepsilon_1^2,
 &\delta&:=q/s^2<1,\\
 p&:=\frac{\varepsilon_1}{s-\varepsilon_1},
 &\beta&:=\varepsilon_2+pM_2.
\end{align*}
For the curvature bound, abbreviate
\[
 r_N:=4M_2\beta+2\beta^2,
 \qquad r_D:=2M_1^2q+q^2.
\]
Then $\widehat F$ is an immersion and the following statements hold.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item The metrics satisfy
  \begin{equation}
   \begin{aligned}
   \norm{\widehat G-G}&\le q,\\
   (1-\delta)G&\preceq\widehat G\preceq(1+\delta)G.
   \end{aligned}
   \label{eq:metric-stability}
  \end{equation}
  Consequently, for every $z,z'$ in the same component,
  \begin{equation}
   \begin{aligned}
   \sqrt{1-\delta}\,d_G(z,z')
   &\le d_{\widehat G}(z,z')\\
   &\le\sqrt{1+\delta}\,d_G(z,z').
   \end{aligned}
   \label{eq:distance-stability}
  \end{equation}
  \item The second fundamental forms obey
  \[
   \max_{i,j}\norm{\widehat B_{ij}-B_{ij}}\le\beta.
  \]
  Moreover,
  \begin{equation}
  \abs{\mathcal K_{\widehat F}-\mathcal K_F}
  \le
  \frac{r_N+2M_2^2r_D/s^4}{(s-\varepsilon_1)^4}.
  \label{eq:curvature-stability}
  \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

The theorem is agnostic to the optimizer and architecture.  It is a transfer
result: any statistical or approximation guarantee for decoder derivatives
immediately becomes a geometric guarantee.  The denominator also identifies a
failure mode: geometry is unstable near rank-deficient decoder Jacobians.

\begin{proof}
Write $J=J_F$, $\widehat J=J+E$.  Expanding
$\widehat J^{\trans}\widehat J-J^{\trans}J$ gives
\[
 \norm{\widehat G-G}\le2\norm{J}\norm{E}+\norm{E}^2\le q.
\]
Since $G\succeq s^2I$, this implies \eqref{eq:metric-stability} as quadratic
forms.  Integrating the square-root bounds along every curve and taking infima
proves \eqref{eq:distance-stability}.  Also
$\sigma_{\min}(\widehat J)\ge s-\varepsilon_1>0$.

Let $P$ and $\widehat P$ be the tangent-space projectors.  The elementary
subspace perturbation bound
$\norm{\widehat P-P}\le\varepsilon_1/(s-\varepsilon_1)=p$ follows by measuring
the distance from a unit vector in either column space to the other space.
Writing $H_{ij}=\partial_i\partial_jF$,
\[
 \widehat B_{ij}-B_{ij}
 =(I-\widehat P)(\widehat H_{ij}-H_{ij})
 +(P-\widehat P)H_{ij},
\]
proves the $\beta$ bound.

Let $N=\langle B_{11},B_{22}\rangle-\norm{B_{12}}^2$.  Projection does not
increase norms, so $\norm{B_{ij}}\le M_2$ and
\[
 \abs{\widehat N-N}\le r_N,
 \qquad \abs{N}\le2M_2^2.
\]
Furthermore, $\det G\ge s^4$, $\det\widehat G\ge(s-\varepsilon_1)^4$, and the
two-dimensional determinant identity gives
\[
 \abs{\det\widehat G-\det G}\le r_D.
\]
Applying these inequalities to
$\widehat N/\det\widehat G-N/\det G$ proves
\eqref{eq:curvature-stability}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Necessity of the regularity hierarchy}

Small value error alone cannot control derivatives, and first-derivative
control alone cannot control curvature.  The next proposition makes both gaps
explicit without leaving the Gaussian decoder class.

\begin{proposition}[Sharpness of the regularity hierarchy]
\label{prop:counterexamples}
On $\cZ=(-1,1)^2$, there are smooth immersed diagonal Gaussian decoders with
the following properties.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item $F_k\to F_0$ uniformly, while the induced metrics do not converge
  uniformly.
  \item $H_k\to H_0$ in $C^1$, while their Gaussian curvatures do not converge
  pointwise at the origin.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
It suffices to specify the mean immersion in $\R^3$ and append a constant
positive standard-deviation vector.  For (a), take
\begin{align*}
 F_k(x,y)&=(x,y,k^{-1}\sin(kx)),\\
 F_0(x,y)&=(x,y,0).
\end{align*}
The value error is at most $k^{-1}$, but at $x=0$ the $(1,1)$ metric entry is
$1+\cos^2(kx)=2$, rather than the limiting value $1$.

For (b), let
\begin{align*}
 H_k(x,y)&=\left(x,y,k^{-2}[\cos(kx)+\cos(ky)]\right),\\
 H_0(x,y)&=(x,y,0).
\end{align*}
The value error is at most $2k^{-2}$ and the Jacobian error at most
$\sqrt2/k$.  Thus $H_k\to H_0$ in $C^1$.  For a graph $(x,y,f(x,y))$,
\[
 \mathcal K=\frac{f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2}
 {(1+f_x^2+f_y^2)^2}.
\]
At the origin, $f_x=f_y=f_{xy}=0$ and $f_{xx}=f_{yy}=-1$, so
$\mathcal K_{H_k}(0,0)=1$ for every $k$, whereas
$\mathcal K_{H_0}(0,0)=0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Piecewise-affine decoders are flat almost everywhere]
\label{cor:relu-flat}
If the parameter map $F=(m,\sigma)$ is a piecewise-affine immersion, then its
pullback curvature is zero in the interior of every linear region and hence
almost everywhere.  This applies to a ReLU network with an affine parameter
output, independently of its value approximation error.
\end{corollary}

Nonlinear output transformations such as $\sigma=\exp(\ell)$ can themselves
create curvature, so Corollary~\ref{cor:relu-flat} concerns the actual parameter
map $F$, not merely its pre-activation.  Our ReLU baseline outputs $\sigma$
directly and enforces positivity with a piecewise-affine clamp.

\subsection{Chord versus intrinsic transport}

The stability theorem controls the path metric.  It does not make the endpoint
Wasserstein distance equal to that path metric.  Their local discrepancy is
controlled by extrinsic bending.

\begin{theorem}[Cubic chord--intrinsic gap]
\label{thm:chord-gap}
Let $\gamma:[0,L]\to\cZ$ be a unit-speed minimizing $G$-geodesic.  If
\[
 \norm{B(\dot\gamma(t),\dot\gamma(t))}\le\Lambda
 \quad\text{and}\quad \Lambda L\le\pi,
\]
then
\begin{equation}
 \frac{2}{\Lambda}\sin\frac{\Lambda L}{2}
 \le \W(K_{\gamma(0)},K_{\gamma(L)})\le L,
 \label{eq:sine-bound}
\end{equation}
with the limiting interpretation when $\Lambda=0$.  Consequently,
\begin{equation}
 0\le L-\W(K_{\gamma(0)},K_{\gamma(L)})
 \le\frac{\Lambda^2L^3}{24}.
 \label{eq:cubic-gap}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

The upper bound is \eqref{eq:chord-intrinsic}.  For completeness, the lower
bound follows because $c=F\circ\gamma$ has unit speed and
$\norm{\ddot c}=\norm{B(\dot\gamma,\dot\gamma)}\le\Lambda$.  Hence the angle
between $\dot c(s)$ and $\dot c(t)$ is at most $\Lambda\abs{s-t}$.  Expanding
$\norm{\int_0^L\dot c(t)\dd t}^2$ as a double integral and lower-bounding the
inner product by $\cos(\Lambda\abs{s-t})$ yields
\eqref{eq:sine-bound}.  The inequality $\sin x\ge x-x^3/6$ gives
\eqref{eq:cubic-gap}.  A circle of radius $1/\Lambda$ attains the sine bound.

\section{Exact Spherical Benchmark}
\label{sec:benchmark}

We construct a benchmark whose observations are distributions, whose visible
coordinates are distorted, and whose complete geometry is known analytically.
This realizes the map analogy literally: equal coordinate displacements on a
flat map need not represent equal travel distances.

Let $u=(\lambda,y)\in[-\pi,\pi)\times[-2.5,2.5]$ be Mercator coordinates and
define the inverse map to the unit sphere by
\begin{equation}
 s(\lambda,y)=
 \bigl(\sech(y)\cos\lambda,\ \sech(y)\sin\lambda,\ \tanh y\bigr).
 \label{eq:mercator}
\end{equation}
At every coordinate, generate a three-dimensional diagonal Gaussian
\begin{equation}
 K_u=\mathcal N\!\left(a s(u),
 \diag\bigl((\sigma_0\mathbf 1+b s(u))^2\bigr)\right),
 \label{eq:benchmark-decoder}
\end{equation}
where $a=0.8$, $b=0.6$, and $\sigma_0=0.75$.  Positivity follows from
$\sigma_0>b$, and $a^2+b^2=1$.  Therefore the true parameter map $F_*$ obeys
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 \W(K_u,K_v)&=\norm{F_*(u)-F_*(v)}\\
 &=\norm{s(u)-s(v)}.
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:exact-sphere-chord}
\end{equation}
The benchmark has the exact answers
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 G_*(\lambda,y)&=\sech^2(y)I_2,\\
 \mathcal K_*&=1,\\
 d_{G_*}(u,v)&=\arccos\langle s(u),s(v)\rangle.
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:benchmark-truth}
\end{equation}
If $d=d_{G_*}(u,v)$, then
\begin{align}
 \W(K_u,K_v)&=2\sin(d/2),\notag\\
 d-\W(K_u,K_v)&=d^3/24+O(d^5).
 \label{eq:sphere-gap}
\end{align}
Thus the sphere attains Theorem~\ref{thm:chord-gap} with $\Lambda=1$.

Consequently, PCA provides a faithful three-dimensional visualization rather

identifiability error: in general, an intrinsic metric does not uniquely
determine an extrinsic decoder without the second fundamental form.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\paragraph{Data.}
We discretize \eqref{eq:mercator} on $72$ longitudes and $41$ Mercator rows,
giving $2{,}952$ states.  At each state we draw $64$ samples from
\eqref{eq:benchmark-decoder}.  Seventy percent of states are used for training
and thirty percent for testing.  The model sees coordinates and sample
sufficient statistics, never $s(u)$, $G_*$, or $\mathcal K_*$.  We report means
and sample standard deviations over five independently generated datasets,
splits, and initializations.

\paragraph{Models and training.}
All decoders are three-layer MLPs of width $96$ and output
$(m_\theta,\sigma_\theta)\in\R^6$.  They differ only in hidden activation:
Tanh, Softplus, or ReLU.  We minimize the exact Gaussian negative
log-likelihood from empirical means and variances using AdamW for $2{,}400$
steps.  Standard deviations are output directly with a positive clamp, keeping
the ReLU parameter map piecewise affine.  Jacobians and Hessians are then
computed by automatic differentiation.  Local intrinsic distance is estimated
by shortest paths on a periodic grid graph with exact $W_2$ edge weights.

\paragraph{Metrics.}
We evaluate held-out parameter RMSE, mean relative Frobenius metric error,
curvature MAE on $\abs y\le2$, geodesic RMSE over $800$ held-out pairs, and PCA
surface RMSE after orthogonal alignment.  The oracle geodesic error measures
grid discretization alone.

\subsection{Likelihood Does Not Fix Curvature}

Held-out NLL is $0.0708\pm0.0028$ for Tanh,
$0.0709\pm0.0028$ for Softplus, and $0.0715\pm0.0030$ for ReLU.  These values
are nearly indistinguishable, and parameter RMSE remains below $0.020$ for all
models.  Nevertheless, Table~\ref{tab:main-results} shows a qualitative
difference in geometry.  Tanh reaches curvature MAE $0.140\pm0.016$, whereas
ReLU returns zero curvature at every evaluation point and therefore MAE exactly
one in all five trials.  This is not an optimization accident: it is the
architecture-level prediction of Corollary~\ref{cor:relu-flat}.

  fields; the ReLU metric is usable but noisy, while its curvature is
  identically zero.}
  \label{fig:geometry}
\end{figure*}

The first-order targets are less fragile.  The ReLU metric error is larger than
that of Tanh, but its geodesic RMSE is still $0.042\pm0.008$.  This is consistent
with Theorem~\ref{thm:stability}: a piecewise-affine function can approximate a
smooth map and its first derivatives sufficiently for distances, while its
classical Hessian remains zero inside every linear region.

\subsection{Local Transport Corrects Chord Bias}

Figure~\ref{fig:distance} separates three objects that are often conflated.
Flat coordinate distance has RMSE $0.854\pm0.035$ against the great-circle
distance.  Direct endpoint $W_2$ is the correct sphere chord but still has RMSE
$0.362\pm0.013$ as an intrinsic-distance estimator.  Summing only local $W_2$
edges from the learned Tanh decoder reduces this to $0.021\pm0.003$, close to
the oracle graph discretization error $0.0148\pm0.0008$.

Finally, the learned Tanh parameter surface has PCA reconstruction RMSE
$0.021\pm0.002$ after rigid alignment.  This supplies the visual ``map to
sphere'' result in Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}, while the metric, curvature, and
distance evaluations specify exactly what geometric recovery means.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}

\paragraph{Implication for generative models.}
The theorem changes the interpretation of decoder accuracy.  Likelihood or
reconstruction is a zeroth-order target.  Geometry-aware interpolation requires
first-order generalization, and curvature-dependent modeling requires
second-order generalization.  Smooth activations are therefore necessary for a
classical curvature field, but not sufficient: the learning procedure must also
control derivative error.  Sobolev regularization, derivative observations, or
architecture-specific derivative bounds are natural ways to instantiate the
premises of Theorem~\ref{thm:stability}.

\paragraph{From a chart to a VAE.}
The benchmark supplies the latent chart in order to isolate decoder geometry.
For a VAE, the same analysis is applied after encoding: $z$ is the learned code
and $F=(m,\sigma)$ is the probabilistic decoder.  When data contain discrete
classes such as MNIST digits, a single smooth manifold need not be appropriate.
A natural extension is a class-conditional or stratified family
$\bigcup_k\cZ_k$, with one pullback geometry per decoder branch.  The present
theory applies within each smooth stratum but does not identify their topology.

\paragraph{Scope.}
Our exact $W_2$ immersion uses diagonal Gaussians.  Full covariance introduces
the Bures--Wasserstein geometry, and non-Gaussian decoders require a different
ambient metric or numerical OT.  The experiment is deliberately controlled:
real datasets do not provide ground-truth curvature, making them unsuitable for
validating a curvature consistency theorem by themselves.  We also do not
claim that the metric determines the extrinsic decoder; recovering an immersion
up to rigid motion generally requires compatible first and second fundamental
forms.  Finally, our theorem transfers derivative accuracy to geometry but does
not by itself prove a neural-network derivative estimation rate.

\section{Conclusion}

Optimal transport gives a coordinate-invariant definition of local decoder
geometry, but it does not make that geometry automatically learnable from a
good reconstruction.  For diagonal Gaussian decoders, we proved the precise
hierarchy: $C^1$ decoder control yields stable metrics and intrinsic distances,
whereas $C^2$ control is required for curvature.  Smooth counterexamples and
the ReLU result show that these levels cannot be collapsed.  The Wasserstein
cartography benchmark makes the distinction visible: local transport corrects
a distorted map and recovers spherical travel, while a model with essentially
the same likelihood can erase curvature completely.  The appropriate
``structure'' of a geometry-aware generative model is therefore not specified
by its latent dimension alone; it includes the differential regularity of its
decoder and the order of geometry the task requires.
