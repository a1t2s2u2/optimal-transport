\begin{abstract}
A two-dimensional latent plot is a distorted map.  It is drawn in the
coordinates an encoder happened to choose, not in the geometry its decoder
actually produces, so equal steps on the plot can move very unequal amounts of
probability mass.  This paper measures that distortion and undoes it.

We pull the decoder's geometry back to a prescribed latent triangulation by
computing 2-Wasserstein distances between decoded measures on mesh edges.  The
resulting piecewise flat metric carries an angle-defect curvature, and this is
the pullback curvature we visualize.  Undoing the distortion means realizing
that metric with coordinates, and we use the smallest ambient dimension that
can carry it: one added dimension, from the flat latent plane to a surface in
$\mathbb R^3$.

Three questions are answered exactly.  How many dimensions are needed is
decided by the rank of a centered Gram matrix.  When the restored shape is
unique is decided by curvature sign: a closed surface with nonnegative
angle defect is rigid by Alexandrov's theorem, while mixed curvature leaves a
bending freedom that no amount of data removes.  How much was actually
restored is decided by facewise singular values and incident angle changes,
both computed from the input and output with no small-error assumption.

We verify recovery on ideal manifolds before applying the construction.  On
convex surfaces, where rigidity makes the answer unique, exact Wasserstein
edge lengths recover the shape to machine precision and noisy ones stay well
inside a rigidity bound.  On a mixed-curvature surface, the intrinsic geometry
is recovered exactly while the embedding is pinned only by a display
convention.  We then apply the same pipeline to a two-dimensional MNIST VAE
using unregularized discrete optimal transport between decoded images.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A latent coordinate is a map coordinate.  Like a flat world map, it is
convenient to draw on and systematically misleading about distance: equal
displacements in different regions of a VAE plot can move very different
amounts of image mass.  A cartographer fixes this by knowing the distortion of
the projection and, when the distortion matters, abandoning the flat sheet for
a globe.  This paper does the same thing for latent spaces.

The distortion is made precise by pullback geometry, which differentiates the
decoder and builds a Riemannian metric from its Jacobian
\cite{arvanitidis2018,chen2018,roy2022}.  We avoid derivatives and instead
observe the same geometry with finite measurements: the 2-Wasserstein distance
between the measures the decoder produces at two nearby latent points.
Collecting these along the edges of a triangulation gives a
\emph{piecewise flat} metric, whose curvature is the angle defect at each
vertex.  That is the pullback curvature, and it is what our figures show.  The
whole construction is one chain,
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 z&\longmapsto K_z
 \longmapsto W_2(K_z,K_{z'})
 \longmapsto \text{piecewise flat metric}\\
 &\longmapsto \text{angle-defect curvature}
 \longmapsto \text{surface in }\mathbb R^3,
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:paper-chain}
\end{equation}
in which the first arrows measure the distortion and the last one undoes it.
Figure~\ref{fig:distortion-teaser} shows both ends of that chain.

Undoing the distortion then means finding coordinates that realize this
metric.  Three questions arise, and each has an exact answer.

\paragraph{How many dimensions?}
We want the flattest honest picture, so we ask for the smallest ambient
dimension that carries the metric.  For a complete distance matrix this is the
rank of a centered Gram matrix (Proposition~\ref{prop:dimension-test}).  A
two-dimensional latent region needs at least three whenever its curvature is
nonzero, and one added dimension is what we use throughout.

\paragraph{Is the restored shape unique?}
Not always, and the dividing line is curvature sign.  If the surface is closed
and every angle defect is nonnegative, Alexandrov's theorem makes the convex
realization unique up to a rigid motion, so \emph{restoring} is literally the
right word.  With mixed curvature or a boundary, the surface can bend without
changing any intrinsic length, and no estimator can select among the bent
copies.  We say exactly which of our results falls on which side.

\paragraph{How much was restored?}
Facewise affine singular values bound every intrinsic distance change, and
incident angle changes bound every curvature-mass change
(Proposition~\ref{prop:pl-certificate}).  Both are computed from the input and
the returned output, with no small-error assumption and no ground truth.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item We define a \emph{Wasserstein latent surface}: the piecewise flat
  metric and angle-defect curvature obtained by pulling the decoder's geometry
  back to a prescribed two-dimensional triangulation using only decoder
  evaluations and OT solves, with no decoder derivatives.
  \item We settle the minimal ambient dimension by a finite rank test, and
  separate the case in which the restored shape is unique (nonnegative
  curvature, closed) from the case in which a bending freedom is unavoidable.
  \item We prove $O(h)$ consistency with the continuous Wasserstein pullback
  distance for smooth diagonal-Gaussian decoders, and give an a posteriori
  distortion audit that holds for any returned realization.
  \item We verify recovery on ideal manifolds first: a convex surface where
  rigidity makes recovery of the \emph{shape} meaningful, then a
  mixed-curvature surface where only the intrinsic geometry is recoverable.
  MNIST is then the application of that verified pipeline.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Geometry of generative models.}
Latent Space Oddity and its successors pull an observation-space metric back
through a decoder and compute geodesics and volume distortion
\cite{arvanitidis2018,chen2018,arvanitidis2022}; Optimal Latent Transport
pulls back Wasserstein geometry instead \cite{roy2022}, and identifiability
results show metric structure can survive when latent coordinates do not
\cite{syrota2025}.  These works stop at the metric.  We take the further step
of realizing it as coordinates, in the smallest dimension that fits, and of
reporting how much the realization distorts.

\paragraph{Learning from distances.}
Isomap recovers coordinates from graph distances \cite{tenenbaum2000}; Wassmap
replaces Euclidean dissimilarity by pairwise Wasserstein distance
\cite{hamm2023wassmap} and LOT Wassmap accelerates it with linearized
transport \cite{cloninger2025}.  Recovering a manifold, or its metric, from
noisy intrinsic distances has finite-sample and asymptotic guarantees
\cite{qiu2023,fefferman2020,hamm2025}.  All of these infer coordinates from
distance data; we keep the latent mesh fixed, specialize the observations to
decoded measures, and audit the returned surface.

\paragraph{Polyhedral realization and rigidity.}
Which piecewise flat metrics are realizable, and how uniquely, is classical.
Cauchy's rigidity theorem and Alexandrov's realization theorem together make a
nonnegatively curved polyhedral metric on the sphere the boundary of exactly
one convex polytope \cite{alexandrov2005,pak2010}, while Burago and Zalgaller
show that every polyhedral metric on an orientable surface, or one with
boundary, embeds isometrically in $\mathbb R^3$ if the triangulation may be
refined \cite{burago1995}.  Facewise singular values are the standard language
for mesh-parameterization distortion \cite{lipman2012}.  We connect these to
decoder-induced Wasserstein edge geometry.

\paragraph{Discrete OT and curvature.}
Discrete Kantorovich transport gives a natural distance between images viewed
as pixel masses \cite{peyre2019}; we use an unregularized linear program
through POT \cite{flamary2021}, so no entropic bias enters.  Angle defect is
the intrinsic curvature mass of a piecewise flat surface; its convergence to
smooth Gaussian curvature needs mesh conditions and is not pointwise reliable
on an arbitrary triangulation \cite{gawlik2020,hartig2021}, so curvature
\emph{mass} is our primary quantity and density is reported only with its
noise scaling made explicit.

\section{The Pullback Metric on a Latent Mesh}
\label{sec:setup}

The decoder does not output points; it outputs probability distributions over
some space $\mathcal Y$ of observations, so we need a distance between
distributions.  We use the $2$-Wasserstein distance, the cost of the cheapest
way to transport one distribution onto the other when moving a unit of mass a
distance $d$ costs $d^2$.  Formally, $(\mathcal Y,d_{\mathcal Y})$ is a
complete separable metric space, $\cP_2(\mathcal Y)$ is the set of Borel
probability measures with finite second moment, and
\begin{equation}
 W_2(\mu,\nu)^2
 =\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)}
 \int d_{\mathcal Y}(y,y')^2\dd\gamma(y,y'),
 \label{eq:w2-definition}
\end{equation}
where $\Gamma(\mu,\nu)$ is the set of couplings of $\mu$ and $\nu$, that is,
joint distributions on $\mathcal Y\times\mathcal Y$ with marginals $\mu$ and
$\nu$ \cite{villani2009,peyre2019}.

Let $\cZ\subset\mathbb R^2$ be a latent region and
\begin{equation}
 K:\cZ\longrightarrow\cP_2(\mathcal Y),\qquad z\longmapsto K_z.
 \label{eq:decoder-measure-map}
\end{equation}
The map may be the conditional distribution of a probabilistic decoder, or a
probability measure that we define explicitly from a decoded object.  The
latter is used for MNIST: normalized grayscale intensities are masses at pixel
locations.  These two interpretations are not conflated.

The decoder induces a \emph{pseudometric} on the latent region, that is, a
distance that satisfies the triangle inequality but may vanish between
distinct points:
\begin{equation}
 d_K(z,z'):=W_2(K_z,K_{z'}).
 \label{eq:decoder-pseudometric}
\end{equation}
It is a metric if $K$ is injective.  Injectivity is not needed below; a zero
edge simply has to be excluded from a nondegenerate triangle.

\subsection{Finite input and angle-defect curvature}
\label{sec:finite-input}

Let $\mathcal T=(V,E,\mathcal F)$ be a finite, connected triangulation of a
compact two-dimensional manifold, possibly with boundary, and associate
$z_i\in\cZ$ and $K_i:=K_{z_i}$ with every vertex.  The unknown exact edge
length and its numerical observation are
\begin{equation}
 \ell_{ij}^{*}=W_2(K_i,K_j),
 \qquad \widehat\ell_{ij}\simeq\ell_{ij}^{*}
 \quad ((i,j)\in E).
 \label{eq:true-observed-lengths}
\end{equation}
Call a positive edge-length assignment $\ell$ \emph{admissible} if the three
lengths on every face satisfy strict triangle inequalities.  An admissible
$\ell$ realizes each face as the Euclidean triangle with those side lengths;
gluing these triangles along shared edges makes $\mathcal T$ a space in which
distance is measured by travelling inside the surface:
$M_\ell=(\mathcal T,d_\ell)$, where $d_\ell(p,q)$ is the infimum of the
Euclidean lengths of paths from $p$ to $q$ that are straight inside each face.
This \emph{intrinsic piecewise flat metric} uses no ambient coordinates.  We
assume $\ell^*$ is admissible; admissibility of the observed and displayed
assignments is audited in Section~\ref{sec:certificate}.

This construction uses direct Wasserstein distances only on mesh edges.  At
nonadjacent vertices, $d_{\ell^*}(i,j)$ is in general neither the direct value
$W_2(K_i,K_j)$ nor a geodesic distance of a continuous decoder pullback
metric; Proposition~\ref{prop:gaussian-mesh-consistency} below closes that gap
only for smooth diagonal-Gaussian immersions.  The finite audit of
Section~\ref{sec:certificate} concerns precisely the edge-$W_2$-induced metric
just defined.

For an admissible $\ell$ and a face $f=(i,j,k)$, the cosine law fixes the
angle at $i$,
\begin{equation}
 \theta_{i,f}(\ell)
 =\arccos\!\left(
 \frac{\ell_{ij}^2+\ell_{ik}^2-\ell_{jk}^2}
 {2\ell_{ij}\ell_{ik}}
 \right),
 \label{eq:finite-angle}
\end{equation}
and $A_f(\ell)$ denotes the Euclidean area of that triangle.  At an interior
vertex, the curvature mass is the angle defect
\begin{equation}
 \kappa_i(\ell)=2\pi-\sum_{f\ni i}\theta_{i,f}(\ell).
 \label{eq:finite-curvature}
\end{equation}
For a boundary vertex, $2\pi$ is replaced by $\pi$; the result is boundary
geodesic-curvature mass, not Gaussian curvature.  We report interior vertices
in the main experiment.  With barycentric vertex area
$A_i=\frac13\sum_{f\ni i}A_f$, one may form the curvature density
$\mathcal K_i=\kappa_i/A_i$, but $\kappa_i$ is the primary finite quantity.

\subsection{Connection to the continuous pullback metric}

Take $\mathcal Y=\mathbb R^n$ with the Euclidean distance.  For a
diagonal-Gaussian decoder with componentwise positive $\sigma(z)$,
\begin{equation}
 \begin{gathered}
 K_z=\mathcal N\!\left(m(z),\diag(\sigma(z)^2)\right),\\
 F(z)=(m(z),\sigma(z))\in\mathbb R^{2n},
 \end{gathered}
 \label{eq:diagonal-gaussian-decoder}
\end{equation}
the Gaussian transport formula gives \cite{givensshortt1984,takatsu2011}
\begin{equation}
 W_2(K_z,K_{z'})=\norm{F(z)-F(z')}.
 \label{eq:gaussian-chord}
\end{equation}
Suppose $F$ is a smooth immersion, meaning that its Jacobian $J_F(z)$ has
rank $2$ everywhere, so $F$ does not collapse any direction.  Measuring
lengths of latent displacements by the lengths of their images under $F$
defines the \emph{pullback metric} $G(z)=J_F(z)^{\trans}J_F(z)$, and for a
displacement $w\to0$,
\begin{equation}
 W_2(K_z,K_{z+w})^2
 =w^{\trans}G(z)w+o(\norm w^2).
 \label{eq:local-w2-expansion}
\end{equation}
Thus local $W_2$ queries are finite observations of the same metric that a
Jacobian calculation would produce.  The a posteriori audit of
Section~\ref{sec:certificate} requires neither Gaussianity nor
differentiability; Equation~\eqref{eq:local-w2-expansion} only motivates why
sufficiently local $W_2$ values are relevant.

The Gaussian case also admits a precise continuous-to-finite statement.  Let
$\Omega\subset\mathbb R^2$ be a compact connected polygonal region and assume
that $F$ extends to a $C^2$ map on a neighbourhood of $\Omega$.  Let $d_G$ be
the \emph{geodesic} distance of $G$ on $\Omega$, that is, the infimum of
$\int\norm{J_F(\gamma(t))\dot\gamma(t)}\dd t$ over paths in $\Omega$; by
Equation~\eqref{eq:gaussian-chord} this is in general larger than the
straight-line distance $\norm{F(p)-F(q)}$.  Consider triangulations
$\{\mathcal T_h\}_{h\downarrow0}$ of $\Omega$ that are conforming, so that two
faces meet in a common vertex, a common edge, or not at all; have maximum
parameter-space diameter $h$; and are uniformly shape-regular, so that the
ratio of circumradius to inradius stays bounded along the family
\cite{ciarlet1978}.  Let $F_h$ be the nodal piecewise affine interpolant of
$F$ on $\mathcal T_h$, that is, the map that agrees with $F$ at every vertex
and is affine on every face.

\begin{proposition}[Gaussian mesh consistency]
\label{prop:gaussian-mesh-consistency}
Assume
\begin{equation}
 \inf_{z\in\Omega}\sigma_{\min}(J_F(z))\ge\mu>0,
 \qquad
 \sup_{z\in\Omega}\norm{D^2F(z)}\le H.
 \label{eq:gaussian-consistency-assumptions}
\end{equation}
For each mesh edge $(i,j)$, set $\ell_{h,ij}=W_2(K_{z_i},K_{z_j})$.  There is
a constant $C_I$, depending only on the uniform shape-regularity bound, such
that if $\eta_h:=C_IHh/\mu<1$ then $\ell_h$ is admissible.  Let
$\iota_h:\Omega\to M_{\ell_h}$ be the piecewise affine homeomorphism that
preserves vertex labels and, inside each face, barycentric coordinates: a
point written as a weighted average of the three vertices is sent to the point
with the same weights in the corresponding glued triangle.  Then
\begin{equation}
 (1-\eta_h)d_G(p,q)
 \le d_{\ell_h}(\iota_hp,\iota_hq)
 \le(1+\eta_h)d_G(p,q)
 \label{eq:gaussian-mesh-consistency}
\end{equation}
for all $p,q\in\Omega$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Taylor expansion after mapping to a shape-regular reference triangle gives the
standard nodal interpolation estimate \cite{ciarlet1978}
$\norm{D F_h-D F}\le C_IHh$ almost everywhere on each face.  Hence, for every
tangent vector $v$, using $\norm{DFv}\ge\mu\norm v$,
\begin{equation}
 \abs{\norm{D F_hv}-\norm{D Fv}}
 \le C_IHh\norm v
 \le\eta_h\norm{D Fv}.
\end{equation}
The reverse triangle inequality also gives
$\norm{D F_hv}\ge(\mu-C_IHh)\norm v>0$, so every affine face image is a
nondegenerate triangle and $\ell_h$ is admissible.
Equation~\eqref{eq:gaussian-chord} shows that the edge lengths of that image
are exactly $\ell_h$, so each glued face of $M_{\ell_h}$ is isometric to the
affine image of the corresponding face under $F_h$.  Consequently the
$M_{\ell_h}$-length of $\iota_h\circ\gamma$ equals
$\int\norm{D F_h(\gamma(t))\dot\gamma(t)}\dd t$.  Integrating the displayed
pointwise inequalities along every rectifiable path in $\Omega$ and taking
infima gives Equation~\eqref{eq:gaussian-mesh-consistency}.
\end{proof}

Neither $F$ nor $F_h$ needs to be globally injective: the compared object is
the abstract glued metric, not the ambient image with intersection shortcuts.
Unlike the a posteriori quantities of Section~\ref{sec:certificate}, the
factor $\eta_h$ is a priori: it needs the derivative bounds $\mu$ and $H$, and
it exploits the Euclidean representation specific to diagonal Gaussians, so it
does not by itself cover the discrete-image MNIST map or a general
Wasserstein-valued decoder.  No pointwise-curvature conclusion follows from
this distance result alone.

\section{Restoring the Distortion in Minimal Dimension}
\label{sec:restore}

Section~\ref{sec:setup} turned the decoder into an intrinsic metric on the
latent mesh.  Restoring the distortion means going back to coordinates: find
points whose Euclidean distances reproduce that metric, in as few dimensions
as possible.

\subsection{How many dimensions are needed}
\label{sec:dimension}

Suppose for a moment that all pairwise distances $D_{ij}=W_2(K_i,K_j)$ are
available.  Then the minimal ambient dimension is decided exactly.  Let
$\Pi=I-|V|^{-1}\mathbf1\mathbf1^{\trans}$ be the centering projection,
$\circ$ the entrywise product, and
\begin{equation}
 B=-\tfrac12\,\Pi\,(D\circ D)\,\Pi.
 \label{eq:centered-distance-gram}
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Minimal Euclidean dimension]
\label{prop:dimension-test}
$D$ is realized exactly by points in $\mathbb R^q$ if and only if $B$ is
positive semidefinite with $\rank(B)\le q$, and the smallest such $q$ is
$\rank(B)$.  In particular one added dimension suffices, and is necessary for
a nonflat metric, exactly when $B\succeq0$ and $\rank(B)=3$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
If the rows of $Y$ are centered points realizing $D$, expanding
$\norm{Y_i-Y_j}^2$ gives $B=YY^{\trans}$, which is positive semidefinite of
rank equal to the dimension of the span of the centered points.  Conversely
factor $B=YY^{\trans}$ through its eigendecomposition; double centering
reverses the expansion and returns $D_{ij}^2=\norm{Y_i-Y_j}^2$
\cite{liberti2014}.
\end{proof}

Two caveats bound what this test does for us.  It needs the complete matrix,
so it is unavailable from local edges alone, and a general Wasserstein metric
need not pass it: if $B$ is indefinite, no Euclidean dimension realizes $D$.
Conversely, exactness is not the only route to a picture.  Burago and
Zalgaller show that any piecewise flat metric on an orientable surface, or on
one with boundary, admits an exact isometric piecewise linear embedding in
$\mathbb R^3$ provided the triangulation may be refined \cite{burago1995}.
One added dimension is therefore always enough in principle; what our fixed,
unrefined mesh buys is a vertex-by-vertex correspondence with the latent plot,
and what it costs is the residual we audit in
Section~\ref{sec:certificate}.

\subsection{When the restored shape is unique}
\label{sec:rigidity}

An intrinsic metric does not by itself determine an embedding: a sheet can
bend without changing any length, angle, or angle defect measured inside it.
Whether this freedom is present is decided by curvature sign.

\begin{proposition}[Rigid case]
\label{prop:rigidity}
Let $\ell$ be an admissible assignment on a triangulation of the sphere with
$\kappa_i(\ell)>0$ at every vertex.  Then $M_\ell$ is isometric to the
boundary of a convex polytope in $\mathbb R^3$, and that polytope is unique up
to a rigid motion.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Away from vertices $M_\ell$ is locally a Euclidean plane, and around a vertex
it closes up after a total angle $\sum_{f\ni i}\theta_{i,f}=2\pi-\kappa_i$, so
it looks like a cone of that angle.  Positive angle defect at every vertex
therefore makes $M_\ell$ a Euclidean cone metric on $S^2$ with every cone
angle below $2\pi$.  Alexandrov's realization theorem
provides a convex polytope inducing it, and his uniqueness theorem, which
contains Cauchy's rigidity theorem, makes it unique up to congruence
\cite{alexandrov2005,pak2010}.
\end{proof}

So on a closed nonnegatively curved surface, ``restoring the distortion'' is
literally correct: the shape is a function of the edge lengths.  This is the
regime of our first experiment.  Outside it, and in particular for a
mixed-curvature surface or a disk with boundary, adjacent triangles can rotate
about a shared edge while every intrinsic quantity stays fixed.  There a
smoothness or height-field constraint selects one representative among many;
it is a display convention, not geometric evidence, and we label it as such
wherever we use one.

\subsection{Fitting the surface}

Let $Q\supseteq E$ be the set of local pairs queried for visualization, with
$\widehat\ell_{ij}>0$ for every $(i,j)\in Q$.  We construct coordinates
$X=(x_i)_{i\in V}$, $x_i\in\mathbb R^3$, by minimizing the dimensionless local
stress
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal L_Q(X)
 =\frac1{|Q|}\sum_{(i,j)\in Q}
 \left(
  \frac{\norm{x_i-x_j}-\widehat\ell_{ij}}
  {\widehat\ell_{ij}}
 \right)^2
 +\lambda\mathcal R_{\rm display}(X).
 \label{eq:display-objective}
\end{equation}
The optional $\mathcal R_{\rm display}$ selects a representative in the sense
of Section~\ref{sec:rigidity}; it does not enter any proposition.  A separate
curvature loss is unnecessary: if the three edge lengths of every face agree,
all face angles and angle defects already agree.

Denote the displayed edge length by
$\widetilde\ell_{ij}=\norm{x_i-x_j}$ and define
\begin{align}
 \varepsilon
 &=\max_{e\in E}\abs{\widehat\ell_e-\ell_e^*},
 \label{eq:ot-error}\\
 \delta
 &=\max_{e\in E}\abs{\widetilde\ell_e-\widehat\ell_e}.
 \label{eq:display-error}
\end{align}
The first is numerical OT approximation error for the fixed measures $K_i$;
the second is observable after visualization.  By the triangle inequality,
$\max_{e\in E}\abs{\widetilde\ell_e-\ell_e^*}\le\varepsilon+\delta$.

\subsection{How much was restored}
\label{sec:certificate}

The scalar $\delta$ is unnecessarily pessimistic.  A sharper certificate is
still finite and directly computable.  For a labelled face $f=(i,j,k)$ and any
admissible $\ell$, put
\begin{equation}
 P_f(\ell)=
 \begin{pmatrix}
  \ell_{ij} & a_f(\ell)\\
  0 & b_f(\ell)
 \end{pmatrix},
 \quad
 \begin{aligned}
 a_f(\ell)&=\frac{\ell_{ij}^2+\ell_{ik}^2-\ell_{jk}^2}
 {2\ell_{ij}},\\
 b_f(\ell)&=\sqrt{\ell_{ik}^2-a_f(\ell)^2}>0.
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:canonical-triangle}
\end{equation}
Admissibility makes $b_f(\ell)$ real and positive, so $P_f(\ell)$ is
invertible.  Its columns place the two edges at $i$ in a canonical Euclidean
triangle.  Write $\sigma_{\min}$ and $\sigma_{\max}$ for the smallest and
largest singular values, that is, the least and greatest factors by which a
linear map can stretch a vector, and define
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 T_f&=P_f(\widetilde\ell)P_f(\widehat\ell)^{-1},\\
 s_-&:=\min_{f\in\mathcal F}\sigma_{\min}(T_f),\\
 s_+&:=\max_{f\in\mathcal F}\sigma_{\max}(T_f).
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:face-distortion}
\end{equation}
Replacing either labelled triangle's planar placement only multiplies $T_f$ by
orthogonal matrices, leaving its singular values unchanged.  At a vertex,
define the angle-change bound
\begin{equation}
 \Delta_i:=\sum_{f\ni i}
 \abs{\theta_{i,f}(\widetilde\ell)-
       \theta_{i,f}(\widehat\ell)}.
 \label{eq:posterior-only-certificate}
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[A posteriori distortion of a fixed realization]
\label{prop:pl-certificate}
Assume $\widehat\ell$ and $\widetilde\ell$ are both admissible.  Let
$\Phi:M_{\widehat\ell}\to M_{\widetilde\ell}$ be the piecewise affine
homeomorphism preserving vertex labels and barycentric coordinates within
corresponding faces.  Then $0<s_-\le s_+<\infty$ and, for every $p,q$ in the
abstract glued complex,
\begin{equation}
 s_-d_{\widehat\ell}(p,q)
 \le d_{\widetilde\ell}(\Phi p,\Phi q)
 \le s_+d_{\widehat\ell}(p,q).
 \label{eq:distance-certificate}
\end{equation}
At every interior vertex,
\begin{equation}
 \abs{\kappa_i(\widetilde\ell)-\kappa_i(\widehat\ell)}
 \le \Delta_i.
 \label{eq:curvature-certificate}
\end{equation}
The curvature inequality also holds at a boundary vertex when both sides are
read as boundary geodesic-curvature mass.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Admissibility makes every $T_f$ invertible.  On each face its singular values
bound the length change of every tangent vector, so any rectifiable path
$\gamma$ satisfies $L(\Phi\gamma)\le s_+L(\gamma)$; taking the infimum over
paths from $p$ to $q$ gives the upper bound.  Since $\Phi$ is a homeomorphism,
every path from $\Phi p$ to $\Phi q$ is $\Phi\gamma$ for some such $\gamma$,
and $L(\Phi\gamma)\ge s_-L(\gamma)\ge s_-d_{\widehat\ell}(p,q)$ gives the
lower bound.  Because $s_\pm$ are global extrema, it does not matter which
incident face is used on the shared edges.  In the curvature difference the
constants $2\pi$, or $\pi$ at the boundary, cancel, and the triangle
inequality over incident angle changes gives
Equation~\eqref{eq:curvature-certificate}.
\end{proof}

This proposition has no small-error or shape-regularity premise: a nearly
degenerate face is exposed by a small $s_-$, or a large $s_+$ or $\Delta_i$.
The distances are those of the abstract glued complexes, so if displayed
triangles self-intersect in $\mathbb R^3$, the intersection does not create a
shortcut between unrelated abstract faces.

Composing with Proposition~\ref{prop:gaussian-mesh-consistency} closes the
chain in the Gaussian case.  Compute $s_\pm$ with $\ell_h$ in place of
$\widehat\ell$, let $\Psi_h:M_{\ell_h}\to M_{\widetilde\ell}$ be the
barycentric piecewise affine homeomorphism, and set
$\Phi_h=\Psi_h\circ\iota_h$.  Then, for all $p,q\in\Omega$,
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 s_-(1-\eta_h)d_G(p,q)
 &\le d_{\widetilde\ell}(\Phi_hp,\Phi_hq)\\
 &\le s_+(1+\eta_h)d_G(p,q).
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:end-to-end-gaussian-display}
\end{equation}
A sequence of displayed metrics therefore converges to $d_G$ only when its
measured facewise factors also approach one; no optimizer guarantee is
asserted here.

To connect the computed lengths to ideal Wasserstein values, suppose that
$\ell^*$ and $\widehat\ell$ both have edges in $[\ell_{\min},\ell_{\max}]$ and
all angles at least $\alpha_0>0$, and write
$\eta_\varepsilon:=\varepsilon/\ell_{\min}$.  On that compact set of triangle
shapes the maps $\ell\mapsto\theta_{i,f}(\ell)$ and $\ell\mapsto P_f(\ell)$
are $C^1$ with $P_f$ uniformly invertible, and angles are scale invariant.
Hence there are constants $C_\theta,C_g$, depending only on
$\ell_{\max}/\ell_{\min}$ and $\alpha_0$, such that on every face
\begin{equation}
 \abs{\theta_{i,f}(\widehat\ell)-\theta_{i,f}(\ell^*)}
 \le C_\theta\eta_\varepsilon,
 \quad
 \norm{P_f(\widehat\ell)P_f(\ell^*)^{-1}-I}
 \le C_g\eta_\varepsilon.
 \label{eq:ot-perturbation}
\end{equation}
Let $d_i$ be the number of faces incident to $i$.  Summing the first estimate
over those faces and combining with
Equation~\eqref{eq:curvature-certificate} gives
\begin{equation}
 \abs{\kappa_i(\widetilde\ell)-\kappa_i(\ell^*)}
 \le \Delta_i+C_\theta d_i\eta_\varepsilon.
 \label{eq:ideal-curvature-certificate}
\end{equation}
The second confines every singular value of
$P_f(\widehat\ell)P_f(\ell^*)^{-1}$ to
$[1-C_g\eta_\varepsilon,1+C_g\eta_\varepsilon]$, so factoring
$P_f(\widetilde\ell)P_f(\ell^*)^{-1}
=T_f\cdot P_f(\widehat\ell)P_f(\ell^*)^{-1}$ and repeating the proof above
gives, for the barycentric map $\Phi^*:M_{\ell^*}\to M_{\widetilde\ell}$ and
all $p,q\in M_{\ell^*}$,
\begin{equation}
 \begin{aligned}
 s_-(1-C_g\eta_\varepsilon)d_{\ell^*}(p,q)
 &\le d_{\widetilde\ell}(\Phi^*p,\Phi^*q)\\
 &\le s_+(1+C_g\eta_\varepsilon)d_{\ell^*}(p,q),
 \end{aligned}
 \label{eq:ideal-distance-certificate}
\end{equation}
provided $C_g\eta_\varepsilon<1$ so the lower factor is positive.  Thus
$(s_-,s_+,\Delta_i)$ certify the computed OT geometry, and a validated OT
error bound $\varepsilon$ extends them to the ideal edge-$W_2$ geometry.
Floating-point evaluations without outward rounding are reported as computed
diagnostics of these bounds, not as formal interval certificates.

On a quasi-uniform mesh of scale $h$ with bounded degree, and if the true,
observed, and displayed triangles remain in one uniformly shape-regular class
with $r:=\varepsilon+\delta$ small, write
$\widetilde\kappa_i=\kappa_i(\widetilde\ell)$ and
$\kappa_i^*=\kappa_i(\ell^*)$.  The same perturbation argument gives
\begin{equation}
 \abs{\widetilde\kappa_i-\kappa_i^*}=O\!\left(\frac{r}{h}\right),
 \qquad
 \abs{\widetilde{\mathcal K}_i-\mathcal K_i^*}=O\!\left(\frac{r}{h^3}\right),
 \label{eq:density-scaling}
\end{equation}
the second from $A_i^*,\widetilde A_i\asymp h^2$,
$\abs{\widetilde A_i-A_i^*}=O(hr)$, and $\abs{\kappa_i^*}=O(1)$.  Under
adversarial fixed-relative edge noise, refinement therefore keeps distance
distortion at the same order while an unsmoothed pointwise curvature heat map
can become worse.  Curvature mass is our primary finite quantity.
